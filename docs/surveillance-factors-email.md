Subject: How our trade-surveillance flags work — the factors we weigh

Hi [Name],

I wanted to give you a clear, non-technical picture of how our trade-surveillance
review decides that a trade is worth a second look. We manage portfolios on behalf
of our client funds, so for every trade we make for them, the system asks two simple
questions: **was the trade clean, and was it a sound decision?** It then looks at the
trade through several independent "lenses" and combines them into a single verdict.

I've attached a one-page diagram that shows the whole picture and how the pieces feed
into each other. Here is the same thing in words.

──────────────────────────────────────────
A) Was the trade CLEAN? — market-integrity lenses
──────────────────────────────────────────
1. Broker & counterparty concentration — Did one broker take an unusual share of our
   flow in a stock, far beyond its normal presence in that name (both on the day and
   over recent months)? A broker that is genuinely the biggest player is unremarkable;
   one that barely trades the name yet takes most of our side is not.

2. Execution quality — Did we fill at a worse price than the market was offering in the
   surrounding minutes (overpaying on a buy, or underselling on a sell)?

3. Cross, wash & churn — Did our own funds trade against each other within minutes, or
   buy and sell the same stock on the same day — i.e. activity that creates volume but
   no real change in position?

4. Market-manipulation context — Was the stock itself being "operated" that day
   (one or two brokers dominating, circular/wash trading, a pump-and-run)? Buying into
   that environment carries extra risk.

5. Post-trade outcome — Did the price move against us straight afterwards (we bought and
   it fell, or sold and it rose)? Persistent adverse moves suggest we traded against
   better-informed counterparties.

──────────────────────────────────────────
B) Was it a SOUND decision? — investment-merit lenses
──────────────────────────────────────────
6. Company fundamentals (as of the trade date) — Was the company financially weak when
   we bought it? We read its bad-loan ratio (NPL), capital strength, profitability and
   returns, how it ranks against sector peers, its profit trend, and whether it looked
   expensive versus peers. Importantly, we use only the results that were public on the
   trade date — no hindsight.

7. Technical entry (the chart that day) — Did we chase the price (buying near a 20-day
   peak, overbought, stretched well above its recent average) rather than entering at a
   sensible level? For a sale, the mirror question: did we sell into a panic low?

(Coming next: two further lenses in the same framework — order intent, i.e. patterns of
placing and cancelling orders, and news & market sentiment around the trade.)

──────────────────────────────────────────
How the factors combine into ONE flag
──────────────────────────────────────────
• Each lens produces a 0–100 "concern" score, and it is side-aware — buying a weak,
  over-extended or concentrated name is concerning, while selling one usually isn't.
  If we don't have data for a lens, it simply steps aside and the others carry the weight.

• The system weighs every lens together in a balanced way, so no single factor — not
  even broker concentration — dominates the story.

• It escalates the alert when several independent lenses point the same way. One weak
  signal is noise; a stressed company, bought near its peak, into concentrated buying,
  that then fell, is a high-conviction flag.

• It is floored on integrity: a healthy balance sheet or a clean-looking chart can never
  "excuse" a wash-trade or concentration signal.

The result is a single verdict (a 0–100 score and a severity band — Low / Moderate /
Elevated / High) plus a short plain-language note: a one-line conclusion, one balanced
bullet per lens with the actual numbers, and the single thing the desk should confirm.
It is always a flag for review, never an accusation — and reviewers can ask follow-up
questions on any factor through the case's built-in assistant.
